Thomas Jefferson (1743–1826) was the third President of the United States (1801–1809), best known as the principal author of the Declaration of Independence. He was a leading figure of the American Enlightenment, an advocate for democracy and individual rights, and a founder of the Democratic-Republican Party, which opposed the Federalists.

Key Highlights

Author of the Declaration of Independence (1776): Jefferson articulated the colonies’ justification for independence, writing the famous line about "life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness."

President (1801–1809): His presidency is most remembered for the Louisiana Purchase (1803), which doubled the size of the U.S., and the Lewis and Clark Expedition, which explored the newly acquired territory.

Virginia Statesman: Before becoming president, he served as Governor of Virginia, the first U.S. Secretary of State under George Washington, and Vice President under John Adams.

Advocate of Education: He founded the University of Virginia in 1819, envisioning it as a place for public education free of church influence.

Philosophy: He believed in limited federal government, an agrarian-based economy, and strong protection of civil liberties—though his views often clashed with Alexander Hamilton’s more centralized, commerce-oriented vision.

Contradictions: While he wrote about liberty and equality, Jefferson enslaved hundreds of people at his plantation, Monticello. Historians often highlight this tension between his ideals and personal practices.
